# Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain Question Answering

Independent audit on 19 November 2026 • 2004.04906v3 · [Paper record](../../sources/arxiv-2004.04906.md)

## Section-by-section review

- **1–3. Problem and method.** The task is to find a passage containing the answer to a factual question. Separate BERT-base models encode the question and passage as 768-dimensional vectors. Their dot product gives the score, allowing the corpus to be indexed in FAISS before a query arrives. Training uses positives for other questions in the same batch and a hard BM25 negative. Top-k measures the share of questions with an answer in at least one of the first k passages. [PDF, p. 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04906v3#page=1) [PDF, p. 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04906v3#page=2) [PDF, p. 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04906v3#page=3)
- **4 and Table 1.** The authors removed tables, lists and other elements from the 20 December 2018 English Wikipedia snapshot, producing 21,015,324 disjoint blocks of about 100 words. Datasets include NQ, TriviaQA, WebQuestions, TREC and SQuAD. For three datasets, the positive passage is the highest-ranked BM25 result containing the answer. Questions with no hit in the top 100 were set aside, narrowing the available training set. [PDF, p. 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04906v3#page=4)
- **5.1, Table 2 and Figure 1.** The study distinguishes single-dataset from joint training, which excludes SQuAD. Single-dataset training beats BM25 on four datasets and falls behind on SQuAD. The authors attribute the exception to questions written from a paragraph and the narrow selection of articles. On NQ dev, training on just 1,000 examples sufficed to beat BM25. [PDF, p. 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04906v3#page=4) [PDF, p. 5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04906v3#page=5) [PDF, p. 6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04906v3#page=6)
- **5.2–5.4, Table 3.** With seven gold negatives, including examples from the same batch raised top-20 on NQ dev from 63.1% to 69.1%. Adding one BM25 negative helped. A second at the same batch size yielded no further gain. The authors also trained on NQ and evaluated transfer to WQ and TREC. Encoding the corpus took 8.8 hours on eight GPUs. Building the FAISS index took a further 8.5 hours, versus about half an hour for the Lucene index. The throughputs concern different implementations and indexing procedures. [PDF, p. 6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04906v3#page=6) [PDF, p. 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04906v3#page=7)
- **6 and Table 4.** A separate BERT reader selects a passage and then the start and end of the answer. In the authors’ configuration, exact match on NQ is 41.5% with DPR and 32.6% with BM25. On SQuAD the respective values are 29.8% and 38.1%. Comparisons with ORQA and REALM also use figures reported in those papers. [PDF, p. 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04906v3#page=7) [PDF, p. 8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04906v3#page=8)
- **7–8 and bibliography.** The authors distinguish standalone retrieval from subsequent reranking, compare dense, sparse and jointly trained methods, and discuss work with generative models. DPR’s observed advantage depends on the dataset and training procedure. The authors released the code and models publicly. [PDF, p. 8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04906v3#page=8) [PDF, p. 9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04906v3#page=9) [Authors’ repository](https://github.com/facebookresearch/DPR)
- **Appendices A–B, Tables 5–6.** On NQ dev, using a BM25-selected context instead of the matched gold passage reduces top-20 accuracy from 78.1% to 77.1%. With NLL, replacing the dot product with L2 distance changes top-20 accuracy from 78.1% to 76.1%. L2 with triplet loss achieves 78.1%. Hyperparameter tuning differed between Table 3 and Tables 5–6. [PDF, p. 12](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04906v3#page=12)
- **Appendices C–D and Table 7.** Two examples illustrate semantic matching for a question about the Irish Sea and the benefit of BM25 lexical matching for a rare character name. The joint-training comparison freezes the passage encoder. On NQ dev, the authors report 39.8 EM for both joint and standard reader training in this comparison. The 41.5 EM in Table 4 comes from a different data split. [PDF, p. 8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04906v3#page=8) [PDF, p. 12](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04906v3#page=12) [PDF, p. 13](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04906v3#page=13)

## Independent verification

Subtracting the BM25 row from the single-DPR row in Table 2 yields top-20 differences of +19.3, +12.5, +18.2, +8.9 and −5.6 percentage points. The abstract’s “9–19” summarizes the rounded positive differences. The Table 2 caption names passages as the denominator. The formal definition in Section 2 uses questions, which is the definition used on the paper card.
