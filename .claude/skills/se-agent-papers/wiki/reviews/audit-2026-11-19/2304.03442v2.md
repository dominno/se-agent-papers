## Simulation, architecture and study conditions

Sections 1–2 and Figure 1 describe the human–computer interaction objective — the perceived believability of interactions. Section 3 and Figures 2–4 present user control, communication and a day in a character’s life. The idea of hosting a Valentine’s Day party was seeded in the organizer’s memory. The simulation then showed how word of the event spread. ## Independent audit of results

Section 6 and Figure 8. One hundred raters ranked responses from five conditions. TrueSkill falls from 29.89 for the full architecture to 26.88 without reflection, 25.64 without reflection and planning and 21.21 after observations are also removed. A human role-player scored 22.95. The human versus full-ablation comparison was the exception to the reported post-hoc significance. Nested ablations cannot estimate all interactions among components. §7 and Figure 9. Of 25 agents, 13 (52%) knew about the party, including its organizer. Five of the 12 invitees attended (41.7%). News of the mayoral candidacy reached 8 of 25 people (32%). An independent check of the denominator for fabricated acquaintances gives 6/453 = 1.32%. The observations span two days of game time. Sections 8–9 discuss simulation cost, excessive politeness, confusion about world states and the risk of attributing human traits to agents. Appendix A describes periodically caching a synthetic character description. Appendix B gives interview questions and sample answers about self-knowledge, memory, plans, reactions and reflection.
