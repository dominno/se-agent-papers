# Do Automatic Test Generation Tools Generate Flaky Tests?

Audit opened on 19 November 2026 for version `2310.05223v1`, 12 pages. [Paper record](../../sources/arxiv-2310.05223.md) · [arXiv record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05223v1) · [Authors, license and checksum](../../raw/metadata/2310.05223.metadata.json)

## Sections, tables, and figures

- **1–2. Background.** The authors distinguish flaky tests, which can produce inconsistent outcomes across runs, from reproducible failures. They discuss existing taxonomies of flakiness and two search-based test generators. [PDF, p. 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.05223v1#page=1) [PDF, p. 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.05223v1#page=2) [PDF, p. 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.05223v1#page=3)
- **3. The method.** The final sample comprised 1,902 Java projects from Maven Central and 4,454 Python projects from PyPI. The authors compared two configurations of EvoSuite 1.2.0 (Table 1) and used the defaults for Pynguin 0.27.0. Generation budgets were two minutes per class and ten minutes per module, respectively. Tests ran 100 times in fixed order and 100 times in random order. Table 2 and Figures 1–3 document counts, selection, project sizes and coverage. [PDF, p. 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.05223v1#page=3) [PDF, p. 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.05223v1#page=4) [PDF, p. 5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.05223v1#page=5)
- **3.5–3.6. Classification and validity.** Of 1,740 tests whose flakiness was independent of execution order, 481 were selected for causal classification. Four authors rated the same 50 examples (Fleiss’ κ = 0.41). One author rated each remaining example. Tool selection, the search budget, and the rarity of some faults limit generalization. [PDF, p. 6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.05223v1#page=6)
- **4.1–4.2. Frequency.** Table 3 distinguishes counts of tests and projects and identifies order-dependent flakiness. For EvoSuite, the proportions are 1.45% without suppression mechanisms and 0.41% with them. A Wilcoxon test on project-level data gives p < 0.001. Figure 4 shows little overlap between projects affected by different types of flaky tests. [PDF, p. 5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.05223v1#page=5) [PDF, p. 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.05223v1#page=7)
- **4.3. Causes.** Table 4 uses a sample of order-independent tests. Of 57 such EvoSuite tests with suppression enabled, 34 were classified as “other.” These comprised 18 checks on the source of an exception and 16 stack overflows. Figures 5–10 illustrate causes including timing assumptions, randomness, an unordered collection, state left by a previous run and changes to a stack trace after optimization. [PDF, p. 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.05223v1#page=7) [PDF, p. 8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.05223v1#page=8) [PDF, p. 9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.05223v1#page=9)
- **5–7. Recommendations, related work and conclusions.** The authors discuss implications for test-generator developers, test users and researchers. The distribution of causes differs between generated and developer-written tests. The authors compare their findings with earlier Randoop and EvoSuite studies. The references link to the data package and conclude the PDF. There is no appendix. ## Independent check and corrections to the paper card

Table 3 reports 3,832 flaky tests out of 264,000 without suppression, compared with 1,285 out of 310,193 with suppression. The relative reduction in the flakiness rate is 1 − 0.41/1.45 = 71.72% using rounded percentages, or 71.46% using the exact counts. The raw count of flaky tests fell by 66.47%. The earlier card confused the reduction in the rate with the reduction in the count. [PDF, p. 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.05223v1#page=7)
